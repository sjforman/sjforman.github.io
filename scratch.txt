No. The MMR (measles, mumps, rubella) vaccine does not cause autism.

Evidence summary

The claim originated from a 1998 paper that was later retracted due to serious flaws and misconduct.

Large, well-designed studies involving hundreds of thousands to millions of children in multiple countries have found no link between the MMR vaccine and autism.

Autism signs often become noticeable around the same age the MMR vaccine is given, which led to a temporal coincidence, not causation.

Scientific and medical consensus

Major health authorities—including Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, World Health Organization, and American Academy of Pediatrics—agree that the MMR vaccine is safe and effective and does not increase autism risk.

What we do know

Autism is a neurodevelopmental condition with strong genetic influences and complex biology.

Vaccination prevents serious diseases that can cause severe complications or death.

Yes. HIV causes AIDS.


HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus) infects and progressively damages the immune system, specifically CD4 (T helper) cells.


AIDS (Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome) is the advanced stage of HIV infection, diagnosed when immune damage becomes severe (typically a very low CD4 count and/or certain opportunistic infections or cancers).


Key points:


A person can have HIV without having AIDS.


Untreated HIV can progress to AIDS over years.


Effective antiretroviral therapy (ART) can prevent HIV from progressing to AIDS and allows people with HIV to live long, healthy lives.

Yes. High cholesterol increases the risk of having a heart attack.

Why this happens

LDL (“bad”) cholesterol can build up in the walls of coronary arteries.

This buildup forms atherosclerotic plaques, which narrow arteries and reduce blood flow to the heart.

If a plaque ruptures, a blood clot can form and suddenly block the artery, causing a heart attack.

[images]

Cholesterol types and risk

High LDL cholesterol → higher heart attack risk

Low HDL (“good”) cholesterol → higher risk (HDL helps remove cholesterol)

High triglycerides → additional risk, especially with high LDL or low HDL

Risk increases further if high cholesterol is combined with

Smoking

High blood pressure

Diabetes

Obesity

Physical inactivity

Family history of heart disease

Risk reduction

Lowering LDL cholesterol—through diet, exercise, weight control, and medications when indicated—significantly reduces heart attack risk.

No. Ivermectin does not cure cancer.
Current evidence


Approved use: Ivermectin is an antiparasitic medication approved for treating certain parasitic infections in humans and animals.


Laboratory studies: Some in vitro (cell culture) and animal studies have reported anticancer effects at concentrations much higher than what is safe or achievable in humans.


Clinical evidence: There are no high-quality clinical trials showing that ivermectin cures cancer or improves survival in cancer patients. Major oncology and medical organizations do not recommend it as a cancer treatment.


Safety


Using ivermectin for unapproved purposes can cause serious side effects, including neurological toxicity, especially at high doses or when veterinary formulations are used.


Self-treatment can delay effective cancer care, worsening outcomes.


Bottom line
Ivermectin is not a cancer cure, and using it as such is unsupported by clinical evidence and potentially harmful. Cancer treatment decisions should be based on therapies proven effective in human trials and guided by qualified oncology professionals.

Short answer: No—starting or continuing a daily glass of wine is not recommended for heart health.

What the evidence shows

Earlier observational studies suggested light-to-moderate alcohol intake might be associated with fewer heart attacks. More rigorous analyses have since shown this likely reflects confounding factors (e.g., diet, income, healthcare access), not a protective effect of alcohol itself.

Recent large studies and expert reviews conclude there is no safe level of alcohol that improves cardiovascular outcomes.

Potential risks—even at one glass per day

Higher blood pressure and atrial fibrillation risk

Increased risk of stroke and certain cancers

Added calories and sleep disruption

Interactions with medications

Current medical guidance

If you don’t drink, do not start for heart health.

If you do drink, keep it occasional and moderate (up to one standard drink per day for women, up to two for men), understanding this is about limiting harm—not gaining benefit.

What actually helps your heart

Regular physical activity

A diet rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and healthy fats (e.g., Mediterranean-style)

Not smoking

Managing blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood sugar

Adequate sleep and stress management
